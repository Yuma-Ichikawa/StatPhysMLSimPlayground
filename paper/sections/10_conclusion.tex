\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The statistical mechanics of learning is usually presented as a body of exact
results waiting to be extended. This paper treats it instead as a
\emph{measurement discipline} that can be applied wherever the exact result is
missing, provided the measurement is done to the standard the discipline
already requires elsewhere: a control grid dense enough to hold a boundary, a
named scale path, an outer disorder ensemble, and a decision procedure willing
to return nothing.

Across \AtlasTotalFamilies{} settings and \AtlasTotalRuns{} trained models, the
procedure returned a spread of outcomes rather than a uniform confirmation. It
found genuinely diverging responses, candidate boundaries whose location it
declined to fix, crossovers that look like transitions until the finite-size
analysis is run, and at least one cell where the order parameter never
separated from its own uncertainty. Those refusals are the evidence that the
positive entries mean something.

Two lessons generalise beyond the specific cells.

The first is that \textbf{design choices routinely masquerade as physics}. A
three-point control grid cannot produce anything but a censored boundary; a
fixed epoch budget makes wider models look less ordered because they are simply
less optimised. Both artefacts appeared in our own work and both survived until
a consistency check between independent finite-size signatures caught them.
Any claim of a phase boundary in a learning system should be accompanied by the
grid density, the optimisation stopping rule, and the disorder ensemble, because
those three choices can produce or destroy the result on their own.

The second is that \textbf{at this replication a single susceptibility peak is
not evidence}. A quarter of the size-curves in this release put their global
maximum on a one-seed excursion whose neighbours sit at the floor. Rejecting
those changed several verdicts, including the one we had written up as the
paper's headline: a drift exponent that appeared to exclude the proportional
scaling $n\propto d$ no longer excludes anything once the spikes are removed.
The finding survives as a question worth a larger seed budget, not as a result.
Anyone reporting a susceptibility peak from a handful of seeds should show that
it is not one run's excursion, because a quarter of the time it will be.

What the atlas offers a theorist is not an explanation but a specification: a
list of settings, each with a measured exponent or boundary, an interval, and
the experiment that would falsify it. Those entries are deliberately
uncomfortable --- several of them say that a widely used framing does not
survive its own finite-size analysis. That is the most useful thing a numerical
map can do for a theory that has not been written yet.
